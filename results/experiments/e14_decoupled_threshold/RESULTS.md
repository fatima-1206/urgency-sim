## What this changes

1. **The ceiling was an implementation artefact, as the paper suspected.** The coupled
   baseline's inability to spend more than ~10 alarms came from tying its firing level to
   the hazard level. A free level reaches 90 alarms at once.
2. **The large capacity advantage was almost entirely that ceiling.** Against the coupled
   baseline the proposed method leads by +0.142 (budget 90) and +0.284 (budget 150).
   Against a decoupled baseline it leads by +0.011 and +0.023. Roughly 92% and 92% of the
   gap disappear.
3. **At budget 90 the two methods tie.** No claim of superiority is supported there.
4. **At budget 150 the evidence is thin and split.** The proposed method is ahead only in
   the unpredictable scenarios (+0.060, +0.066, both CIs excluding zero) and level or
   slightly behind in the predictable ones (-0.025, -0.010, CIs spanning zero). It also
   spends 1.09x the baseline's alarms, at the edge of the matched band, so this is not a
   clean airtime-matched comparison.
5. **The equal-airtime result is untouched.** At budget 11 the baseline wins in both forms.

**Consequence for the paper.** The capacity claim cannot stand as "the baseline cannot be
matched and loses". The defensible statement is: a coupled baseline saturates; a decoupled
one does not, and at 90 alarms it ties the proposed method, with a possible modest edge for
the proposed method at 150 in unpredictable scenarios. The paper's title and §5.6 depend on
this, and the proposed method's value is better framed as the routing result and the
equal-airtime analysis than as capacity.

Not tested: a decoupled baseline at other hazard levels, other speeds, or other k.
