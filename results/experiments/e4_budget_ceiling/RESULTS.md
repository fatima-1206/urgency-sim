### Verdict

**The ceiling is real and it is ~146–158 alarms per run.** Budget 180 returns the
trigger, the alarm count, the on-time ratio and the latency of budget 150 to the last
decimal, and calibration flags it `-17% OFF TARGET`. The trigger at that point is
0.0156, effectively zero: the method is firing on everything it can and still cannot
reach 180.

The bound is structural, not a tuning limit. The `k_consistent = 3` gate admits at most
one alarm every three ticks, so an 800-tick mission caps out near 267 candidate alarms
before the hazard and observation filters take their share.

Phase 3 stopped at 90 alarms/run and reported on-time "still climbing (0.533, no
plateau)". That was correct, and this extends it: on-time keeps climbing to **0.747**
(predictable/dense) and latency keeps falling to **46.1 ticks**, a 36% reduction against
budget 90. The method is **not** saturated at the budget Phase 3 tested — Phase 3 simply
stopped too early.

### Cost of those extra alarms

Going from 90 to 150 alarms/run buys +0.132 on-time and −25.5 ticks of latency for
**+8.2% energy** (1452 → 1572). Energy is dominated by flight, not transmission, so
alarms are close to free in this simulator. That is a property of the energy model and
should be stated as such rather than claimed as an operational result — a radio with a
realistic transmit cost would change this number.

### One caveat on the sparse latency figures

Under sparse connectivity some runs deliver nothing at all, leaving latency undefined.
That count **rises with budget** (2 → 3 → 9 runs out of 100 from budget 90 to 150), so
the sparse latency means are taken over a shrinking and non-random subset. The sparse
latency improvement is therefore an upper bound on the real one. The dense columns have
no undefined runs and carry the claim.
